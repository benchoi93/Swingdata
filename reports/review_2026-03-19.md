### Suggested Research Directions

1. **Individual-Level Quantile Treatment Effects via Panel QTE (Feasibility: MEDIUM)**
   - Method: The current Distributional TWFE DiD (Appendix C) estimates effects at city-level quantiles, which is a descriptive aggregation. Extend to individual trip-level quantile treatment effects using the Callaway and Li (2019, Journal of Econometrics) panel quantile DiD estimator, implemented in the R package `qte` or approximated via within-city trip-level regressions in Python/statsmodels. The key advantage is that individual-level QTE provides formally identified distributional causal effects under parallel trends at each quantile -- not just city-level distributional heterogeneity.
   - Data needed: `data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet` (21M trips with all outcomes), `data_parquet/v2/city_month_panel.parquet` (treatment timing). Already available. A 5M-trip subsample would be sufficient for estimation.
   - Expected finding: The right-tail compression of mean speed should be stronger at the individual level than at the city level (city aggregation smooths individual heterogeneity). The cruise fraction p90 effect (+0.36) should replicate and may be larger, revealing which trips drive the tail effect. Harsh event QTE would require a Tobit/hurdle approach given zero-inflation.
   - Target venue: Analytic Methods in Accident Research (revision/companion paper) or Journal of Econometrics (applied)
   - Connection to current work: Appendix C explicitly caveat that the city-level quantile approach is "not a formal QTE estimator in the sense of Firpo et al. (2009)." This direction closes that gap directly, upgrading the appendix's descriptive finding into a formally identified causal result. The methodological contribution would be novel in the micromobility literature, which has not yet applied panel QTE methods.

2. **Spatial Diffusion of Behavioral Change Under TUB Ban (Feasibility: HIGH)**
   - Method: Use the city-month panel (`data_parquet/v2/city_month_panel.parquet`) and the 52-city OSM network data to model spatial spillovers in behavioral response. Fit a Spatial Durbin TWFE model: does the TUB ban's effect on harsh events in one city predict changes in neighboring cities? Compute city-level spatial weight matrices using geographic proximity (K-nearest neighbors or inverse distance) and test for spatial autocorrelation in the DiD residuals using Moran's I. This tests whether reduced aggressive riding "spills over" geographically -- for example, because users commute across city boundaries.
   - Data needed: `data_parquet/v2/city_month_panel.parquet` (53 cities x 11 months), city centroid coordinates (from `CITY_CENTERS` dict in config), OSM networks (`data_parquet/osm_networks/`). All available. Spatial weight matrix construction requires `libpysal` (already installed v4.14.1).
   - Expected finding: Near-zero spatial spillovers for within-city safety outcomes (speed governors only affect TUB riders at the point of governance). Possible positive spillovers for demand outcomes (trip counts) if users in neighboring cities observe or discuss the platform restriction. The test would be a meaningful robustness check for the main DiD: if there are no spatial spillovers, the SUTVA assumption is supported.
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C or Analytic Methods in Accident Research
   - Connection to current work: The current paper assumes SUTVA (stable unit treatment value assumption) implicitly. Explicitly testing and confirming no spatial spillovers would strengthen the causal identification claim. The 52-city coverage with dense geographic clustering (Korean cities) makes this dataset unusually well-suited for spatial panel analysis. The `libpysal` and `esda` packages needed for this are already installed.

3. **Behavioral Trajectory Clustering with Sequence-Level Features (Feasibility: HIGH)**
   - Method: For each user's first 50 trips (where TUB adoption ramps from 11.5% to 47.8%), extract a feature vector: (trip rank, mode chosen, harsh events, speed CV, cruise fraction, road class). Apply Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) clustering or a Hidden Markov Model (HMM via `hmmlearn`) to group users by their behavioral trajectory over the first 50 trips. Target 4-6 clusters: "early TUB adopters who escalate," "steady STD users," "ECO switchers," etc. Then test whether the TUB-ban's December 2023 effect on safety outcomes differs across trajectory clusters -- do early TUB adopters show the largest safety gains?
   - Data needed: `data_parquet/all_months/user_longitudinal.parquet` (1.82M users with trip histories), `data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet` (per-trip outcomes). User-trip join needed. A 200K-user subsample for clustering, then apply cluster labels to full panel for heterogeneous treatment effect analysis.
   - Expected finding: Users who adopt TUB early and maintain high TUB usage through November 2023 show the largest December safety improvement (most exposed to the governor removal). Users who never used TUB show null DiD effects -- the natural placebo group within the treatment period. This heterogeneous treatment effect analysis would reveal which "behavioral types" of riders are most affected by speed governance, with direct policy implications for targeted interventions.
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C or IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems
   - Connection to current work: The current paper's Figure 7 (experience trajectories) shows the aggregate TUB adoption curve and its safety consequences. This direction individualizes that analysis: instead of one aggregate curve, show the distribution of user trajectories and how they predict differential causal effects of the TUB ban. This directly leverages the 24-month longitudinal depth of the dataset, which no other e-scooter dataset in the literature can match.
