### Issues Found

1. **[MINOR] Terminology inconsistency: concave vs. accelerating sharply**
   - Location: results.tex, experience-mediation paragraph (around line 215) and discussion.tex Section 5.4
   - The results section describes the TUB adoption curve as featuring an acceleration that is sharp in the first 20-30 trips, while the discussion describes it as a concave adoption curve. A concave function has a *decreasing* rate of change (decelerating), which is the opposite of accelerating sharply. The curve described (rapid early growth followed by plateau) is indeed concave -- so the phrase about acceleration being sharp is the error.
   - Suggested fix: Replace the phrasing with increases rapidly or rises steeply in discussion.tex line 34. The concave characterization is correct.

2. **[MINOR] Robinson (1996) citation precision**
   - Location: background.tex Section 2.4 (line 34)
   - The text states helmet laws were associated with significant reductions in cycling participation. Robinson (1996) specifically reports a 29-36% reduction in cycling numbers in Australian states after mandatory helmet legislation, but the paper also discusses measurement challenges (whether the decline was pre-existing trend vs. law-caused). The current phrasing is appropriately cautious, but adding the specific percentage would strengthen the claim.
   - Suggested fix: Consider adding the 29-36% reduction figure with the caveat about pre-existing trends, or keep current phrasing if brevity is preferred.

3. **[MINOR] Limitations paragraph is a single ~200-word block**
   - Location: discussion.tex Section 5.6 (line 50)
   - The limitations section contains 8 distinct points compressed into one paragraph. At ~200 words, this is readable but dense. For AMAR, numbered or itemized limitations are common and improve scanability.
   - Suggested fix: Either (a) break into 2-3 paragraphs grouped thematically (data limitations, methodological limitations, scope limitations), or (b) use a numbered list. This is a formatting preference, not a content issue.

### Suggested Research Directions

1. **Time-Varying Cox PH with Piecewise Hazards** (Feasibility: HIGH)
   - Method: Replace the standard Cox PH model with a piecewise-constant hazard model or an accelerated failure time (AFT) model with Weibull/log-logistic distribution. The Schoenfeld test violations (5/6 endpoints) indicate that the TUB effect is not constant over the trip sequence. A time-varying coefficient Cox model (using lifelines CoxTimeVaryingFitter or statsmodels parametric survival) would directly estimate how the TUB hazard ratio changes across the trip sequence -- e.g., whether TUB effect is strongest in early trips (trips 1-20) or intensifies later.
   - Data needed: data_parquet/v2/user_longitudinal.parquet (already available). Need to restructure into start-stop format for time-varying covariates.
   - Expected finding: The TUB hazard ratio for first_speeding (currently HR=5.71 average) likely increases from ~3 at trip 1 to ~8+ at trip 50, consistent with the adoption curve. This would provide direct evidence for the escalation intensifies narrative.
   - Target venue: AMAR (methodological contribution) or Accident Analysis and Prevention (applied contribution)
   - Connection to current work: Directly addresses the PH violation limitation flagged in this session. Transforms a limitation into a finding.

2. **Synthetic Control DiD for City-Level Governor Effects** (Feasibility: MEDIUM)
   - Method: Instead of TWFE DiD (which assumes parallel trends and homogeneous treatment effects), construct synthetic control units for each treated city using pre-treatment outcome trajectories from the Feb-Nov 2023 panel. This addresses the pre-trend concerns noted in the event study. The Synth method (Abadie, Diamond, Hainmueller 2010) or the augmented synthetic control method (Ben-Michael, Feller, Rothstein 2021) would provide city-specific treatment effect estimates with valid inference.
   - Data needed: data_parquet/v2/city_month_panel.parquet (already available, 53 cities x 11 months). Need Dec 2023 post-treatment data (already in panel).
   - Expected finding: Cities with high pre-ban TUB share (>50%) show larger treatment effects than TWFE estimates suggest, because TWFE averages across heterogeneous cities. Synthetic control would reveal that some cities (e.g., those with cycling infrastructure) show smaller effects, suggesting infrastructure moderates governor effectiveness.
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C (causal methods + policy) or Journal of the American Statistical Association (methods)
   - Connection to current work: Strengthens the causal identification strategy. The current TWFE design is valid but the synthetic control adds robustness against the pre-trend concern flagged in limitations.

3. **E-Bike vs. E-Scooter Speed Behavior Comparison on Shared Road Segments** (Feasibility: HIGH)
   - Method: The dataset contains 11.3% BIKE_TUB/BIKE_STD trips that are currently unanalyzed. Match e-bike and e-scooter trips on the same H3 hexagon, same time-of-day, and same day-of-week using propensity score matching or coarsened exact matching. Compare speed profiles, harsh event rates, and speed variability across vehicle types on identical road segments.
   - Data needed: data_parquet/all_months/routes_all.parquet (already available, contains BIKE_TUB and BIKE_STD modes). Need to filter and match with scooter trips.
   - Expected finding: E-bikes show lower harsh event rates but potentially higher mean speeds (larger wheels, more stable platform). The TUB/STD mode effect may be attenuated for e-bikes because the speed governor binds less (e-bikes have different speed-effort curves). This would demonstrate that governor effectiveness is vehicle-type-dependent.
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part D (mode comparison) or Journal of Transport Geography (spatial matching)
   - Connection to current work: Exploits the largest untapped data asset in the project. The 11.3% BIKE trips are mentioned in MEMORY.md as largely unanalyzed. A cross-mode comparison would strengthen the generalizability discussion and could become a standalone companion paper.
