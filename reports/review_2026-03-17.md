### Issues Found

1. **[MINOR]** Script line count discrepancy: report states qte_exploration.py is 250 lines but the actual file is 393 lines.
   - Suggested fix: Correct to 393 lines in the report.

2. **[MINOR]** QTE terminology: The analysis is described as Quantile Treatment Effects (QTE) but the implementation is a distributional TWFE DiD -- running separate OLS regressions on city-month quantile summaries (p25, p50, p75, p90) with cluster-robust SEs. Formal QTE methods (Firpo-Fortin-Lemieux 2009 RIF-regression, Callaway-Li 2019 panel QTE) estimate treatment effects at quantiles of the unconditional outcome distribution, which requires individual-level data and recentered influence functions. The city-level aggregation approach is a valid and informative analysis but should be labeled Distributional TWFE DiD or Quantile-level TWFE DiD rather than QTE to avoid methodological confusion at review.
   - Suggested fix: Rename to Distributional TWFE DiD in both the report and the script docstring. If this analysis is included in the paper or a supplement, add a footnote distinguishing it from formal QTE estimators.

3. **[MINOR]** Cruise fraction tail ratio (8.44x) is computed as p90 beta / mean beta = 0.363 / 0.043, but the mean beta has p=0.17 (non-significant). Reporting a ratio with a non-significant denominator is misleading -- it implies the mean effect is real but small when it is actually indistinguishable from zero. The correct interpretation is: The TUB ban has no detectable mean effect on cruise fraction but produces a large positive effect at the 90th percentile.
   - Suggested fix: Report the p90 effect as a standalone finding rather than as a ratio to the non-significant mean. Alternatively, note that the ratio is illustrative and the denominator is non-significant.

### Recommendations

1. **Correct the script line count** from 250 to 393 in the report.

2. **Rename the QTE analysis** to Distributional TWFE DiD throughout. If this work is developed further for publication, consider implementing a formal panel QTE estimator (Callaway-Li 2019) on the individual trip-level data to obtain true unconditional quantile treatment effects. The current city-level approach is a useful descriptive complement but does not estimate the same estimand.

3. **Reframe the cruise fraction finding** to emphasize the p90 standalone effect rather than the tail ratio. The statement while the mean is null, the 90th percentile shows a +0.36 increase is both more accurate and more impactful than a ratio with a non-significant denominator.

4. **Consider adding the distributional analysis to the AMAR paper as a supplementary appendix.** The mean speed distributional gradient (p90 effect 2.1x larger than p50) strengthens the policy narrative: speed governors disproportionately compress the dangerous right tail of the speed distribution, which is exactly where crash risk is highest per the power model (Elvik 2013).

5. **For the pre-submission checklist**, note that the 299 DPI figure resolution is technically below 300 DPI. While this is negligible (0.03% difference) and will not cause any quality issues, if the journal is strict about the 300 DPI threshold, regenerating figures with explicit dpi=300 (rather than relying on matplotlib default rounding) would prevent any automated rejection.

### Suggested Research Directions

1. **Individual-Level Panel Quantile Treatment Effects** (Feasibility: HIGH)
   - Method: Apply Callaway-Li (2019) or Firpo-Fortin-Lemieux (2009) RIF-regression to estimate unconditional quantile treatment effects of the December 2023 TUB ban on individual trip-level outcomes. Use the 44.1M trip dataset with user fixed effects. Compare formal QTE estimates to the city-level distributional TWFE results already obtained.
   - Data needed: data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet (already available, 21M trips Feb-Nov); extend to include Dec 2023 trips. Columns: mean_speed, cruise_fraction, harsh_accel_count, harsh_decel_count, city_id, month, user_id.
   - Expected finding: The formal QTE estimates will confirm the distributional heterogeneity pattern (larger effects at upper quantiles) but with narrower confidence intervals due to individual-level variation. The p25 mean speed effect, currently null at city level, may become significant at individual level due to increased statistical power.
   - Target venue: AMAR supplementary analysis or standalone note in Journal of Safety Research.
   - Connection to current work: Directly extends the city-level distributional TWFE DiD (today) to the methodologically rigorous individual-level estimator, addressing the terminology concern raised in this review. Recent work by Dills and Mulholland (2024, Accident Analysis and Prevention) on e-scooter injury severity used quantile regression; this would be the first QTE application to speed governance behavioral outcomes.

2. **Experience-Stratified Spatial Spillover Analysis** (Feasibility: HIGH)
   - Method: Combine the experience-speeding escalation pathway (trip rank to TUB adoption to speeding) with spatial analysis (H3 hex-level speeding rates). Test whether experienced riders create spatial spillover effects: do hexagons with high concentrations of experienced riders have elevated speeding rates among novice riders in adjacent hexagons? Use spatial Durbin models (SDM) with experience composition as the key regressor and queen contiguity spatial weights.
   - Data needed: data_parquet/spatial/*.parquet (H3 hex aggregates, already available), data_parquet/modeling/trip_experience.parquet (trip rank, already available). Merge to create hex-level experience composition (fraction of trips by riders with 50+ trips). OSM networks already available for 50 cities.
   - Expected finding: Positive spatial spillover of experience-driven speeding. Novice riders in hexagons adjacent to high-experience hexagons speed more than novices in isolated hexagons, suggesting social learning or infrastructure-driven risk normalization. The spillover effect is likely mediated by road type (stronger on arterials where experienced riders cluster).
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C or Spatial Statistics.
   - Connection to current work: Bridges two key findings from the paper -- the experience-mediation result (57-70% of speeding increase mediated by TUB adoption) and the spatial hot-spot analysis (Moran I = 0.21-0.71). Neither finding has been connected to the other. This direction asks whether the escalation pathway has a spatial dimension.

3. **Counterfactual Speed Profile Generation via Conditional Diffusion Models** (Feasibility: MEDIUM)
   - Method: Train a conditional diffusion model (DDPM or flow matching) to generate realistic within-trip speed profiles conditioned on trip metadata (mode, road composition, distance, time of day, user experience). Then generate counterfactual profiles: What would this TUB trip look like under STD mode? Compare generated counterfactual harsh event rates to observed STD rates for causal validation.
   - Data needed: Raw speed vectors from data_parquet/all_months/routes_all.parquet (speeds column, variable-length sequences). Trip metadata from data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet. Requires GPU training (8-24 hours on A100).
   - Expected finding: The diffusion model learns the mode-conditional speed distribution accurately (FID/MMD metrics). Counterfactual TUB-to-STD profiles show harsh event reductions consistent with the DiD estimates (providing an independent validation). The generated profiles also enable privacy-preserving data sharing -- synthetic speed profiles that preserve statistical properties without revealing individual trajectories.
   - Target venue: NeurIPS Workshop on AI for Transportation or TR Part C (leveraging PI expertise in TrajGAIL and deep generative models for transportation).
   - Connection to current work: The paper establishes that speed governors reshape behavioral profiles across multiple dimensions. This direction asks whether a generative model can learn and reproduce those reshaping patterns, enabling counterfactual policy simulation beyond the single natural experiment observed. It directly leverages the PI published expertise in trajectory generation (TrajGAIL, Choi et al. 2021 TR-C) and the generative models tutorial (Choi et al. 2025 TR-C).
