### Suggested Research Directions

1. **Formal Panel Quantile Treatment Effects via Callaway-Li (2019)** (Feasibility: MEDIUM)
   - Method: Apply the Callaway and Li (2019, Journal of Econometrics) panel quantile DiD estimator, which provides formal identification of distributional treatment effects under parallel trends at each quantile. Unlike the current city-level quantile TWFE, this operates at the individual trip level and provides valid inference under weaker assumptions. The R package qte or a Python implementation would be needed.
   - Data needed: Individual trip-level outcomes from data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet (21M trips), city-level treatment intensity from data_parquet/v2/city_month_panel.parquet. Already available.
   - Expected finding: The right-tail compression effect for mean speed should be even stronger at the individual level than at the city level. The cruise fraction hidden tail effect should replicate. Additionally, individual-level QTE may reveal heterogeneous effects masked by city-level aggregation.
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C or Journal of Econometrics (applied)
   - Connection to current work: Directly extends the Distributional TWFE DiD appendix (today addition) from a descriptive complement to a formally identified causal estimand. The current appendix explicitly notes this limitation and cites Firpo et al. (2009), making this the natural next step.

2. **Causal Forest for Heterogeneous Treatment Effect Discovery** (Feasibility: HIGH)
   - Method: Apply Athey and Wager (2018) causal forests (via econml or grf R package) to estimate conditional average treatment effects (CATE) of the TUB ban across user and trip characteristics. Use the Dec 2023 ban as the treatment, with pre/post trip outcomes. The causal forest would discover which subgroups experienced the largest (or smallest) behavioral changes -- e.g., do experienced riders on major roads show larger harsh event reductions than novice riders on residential streets?
   - Data needed: data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet for trip features, data_parquet/v2/city_month_panel.parquet for treatment timing. User demographics from data_parquet/all_months/user_longitudinal.parquet. All already available.
   - Expected finding: Treatment effect heterogeneity concentrated along experience (high-experience TUB users show largest safety gains) and road type (major roads show strongest speed reduction but residential roads show largest harsh event reduction). Age effects likely small after conditioning on experience.
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C or NeurIPS (Causal ML workshop)
   - Connection to current work: The current paper documents the average treatment effect across multiple outcomes. Causal forests would decompose this into subgroup-specific effects, answering the policy-relevant question: who benefits most from speed governors?

3. **Hidden Markov Model for Riding Regime Discovery on Speed Profiles** (Feasibility: HIGH)
   - Method: Fit a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with 3-5 latent states to within-trip speed sequences (variable-length time series from the speeds column). States would correspond to behavioral regimes: cruising, accelerating, braking, stopped, speeding. Use the Baum-Welch algorithm via hmmlearn. Then analyze how regime transition probabilities differ by mode (TUB vs STD), road type, and experience level.
   - Data needed: Raw speed profiles from data_parquet/v2/trip_modeling.parquet (the speeds column contains per-trip speed vectors). A subsample of 200K-500K trips would suffice for estimation. Already available.
   - Expected finding: TUB mode trips will show a dominant high-speed cruising state with sharp transitions (due to the governor ceiling), while STD/ECO trips show more gradual speed transitions. Experienced riders will have longer dwell times in the cruising state regardless of mode. The HMM framework provides a richer characterization of within-trip dynamics than the current summary statistics (cruise fraction, speed CV).
   - Target venue: Transportation Research Part C or IEEE Transactions on ITS
   - Connection to current work: The current paper uses trip-level summary statistics (cruise fraction, speed CV, harsh event counts) to characterize riding behavior. HMMs would operate on the raw speed sequences, capturing temporal dynamics that summary statistics miss. This directly builds on the PI expertise in deep generative models (TrajGAIL) and trajectory analysis. The cruise fraction finding (null mean but large p90 tail effect) suggests that within-trip dynamics are more complex than averages reveal -- HMMs would formalize this intuition.
